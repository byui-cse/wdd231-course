# Passing Off a Competency or Slice

## What is a pass-off?

A pass-off is a short conversation and demonstration in which you show that you can perform, explain, and adapt an entire competency. Learning contracts may focus on individual slices, but the instructor or TA pass-off happens only after you have reviewed every slice of that competency with a peer.

You do not have to complete an entire competency in one learning contract. Your contracts should identify the specific slices you are working on. For example, contracts might separately address:

- creating a semantic page structure,
- handling loading and error states for an API request,
- making a page reflow at different viewport sizes, or
- explaining and fixing a real JavaScript bug.

A working feature is important, but it is only part of the evidence. After all slices of a competency have been discussed with a peer, the formal pass-off is an opportunity to show that you can bring those slices together, explain your decisions, and respond to a small change.

Use the [Competency Progress Tracker](../../competency-progress-tracker.md) to record each peer review, evidence link, and competency-level pass-off.

## The two-stage model

### Stage 1: Peer review of every slice

Before requesting a formal pass-off, review each slice of the competency with a classmate. You should demonstrate or explain evidence for every slice, discuss your challenges and successes, and use your peer's questions to find gaps.

The peer review is complete when:

- every slice in the competency has been addressed,
- you have evidence or a clear explanation for each slice,
- your peer has had an opportunity to ask questions, and
- you have recorded any gaps or next steps.

The peer does not certify the competency. They help you prepare for the certification conversation.

### Stage 2: Instructor or TA pass-off

Once all slices have been reviewed with a peer, request the formal pass-off. The instructor or TA will evaluate the competency as a whole. You may be asked to connect multiple slices, explain tradeoffs, demonstrate a representative feature, or respond to a transfer question.

## The pass-off process

Complete these steps in order:

1. **Review your contracts.** List every slice of the competency and connect each one to its success criteria and evidence.
2. **Prepare your evidence.** Make sure the relevant code or artifacts work, and gather tests, screenshots, notes, or other evidence for every slice.
3. **Teach a peer.** Meet with a classmate and explain each slice, including your challenges, successes, and important decisions.
4. **Use questions to find gaps.** Invite your peer to ask questions about every slice. Treat uncertainty as useful information about what to review.
5. **Complete the peer review.** Record the peer conversation and any missing evidence or follow-up work.
6. **Make final improvements.** Revise your work or explanation based on the conversation. Update your contracts if the scope or evidence changed.
7. **Request the formal pass-off.** Meet with the instructor or TA to demonstrate the competency as a whole, explain your reasoning, answer questions, and complete a small variation or transfer task.
8. **Record the result.** Mark the entire competency as passed, not yet passed, or ready for another attempt. Note the next step in your learning record.

The peer conversation comes before the instructor or TA meeting because explaining every slice is a form of preparation, not an extra assignment. It gives you a low-risk chance to discover what you understand and what still needs attention before you present the complete competency.

## Prepare your evidence

Before beginning the peer review, prepare:

- Your [Competency Progress Tracker](../../competency-progress-tracker.md), updated with the slices you plan to review
- A list of every slice in the competency
- The learning contract or a link to the relevant section for each slice
- The working artifacts, project pages, code, or other deliverables
- A brief explanation of what you intended to learn for each slice
- At least one challenge, success, and important decision from the set of slices
- At least one test, experiment, inspection, or verification result for each relevant slice
- A small change that connects two or more slices and that you believe you could handle

Avoid preparing by memorizing a script. Your goal is to understand the work well enough to explain it in your own words and investigate it when something changes.

## Have the peer conversation

Find a classmate who is willing to listen actively and ask questions. The peer does not need to be an expert or know all the answers. Their job is to help you explain your thinking clearly and notice places where your explanation is incomplete.

Use this structure:

### 1. State the goal

Explain:

- Which competency or slice you are working on
- What you intended to be able to do
- How the work connects to your project or goals

### 2. Demonstrate the work

Show the feature or artifact working. Include at least one normal case and, when relevant, an edge case such as empty data, invalid input, a narrow viewport, or a failed request.

### 3. Explain the learning

Describe:

- What you learned
- What you already knew before starting
- What was difficult or surprising
- What you tried first
- What changed after feedback, testing, or investigation
- What you would do next with more time

### 4. Invite questions about every slice

Ask your peer to choose questions from the list below. Encourage questions about why, not just what.

### 5. Summarize the next step

At the end of the conversation, write down one thing to review and one thing you are ready to demonstrate during the pass-off.

## Sample questions for the peer

The peer can choose a few questions that fit the competency. They do not need to ask every question.

### Questions for any competency

- What were you trying to learn or improve?
- What part of the work are you most confident explaining?
- What part is still difficult to explain?
- What choice did you make that had more than one reasonable option?
- What evidence shows that your solution works?
- What did you try when you got stuck?
- What changed because of testing or feedback?
- What would break first if the requirements changed?
- What small variation could you handle now?

### Questions about HTML and accessibility

- Why did you choose these HTML elements instead of similar-looking elements?
- How would a keyboard user move through this page or interaction?
- What would a screen reader user learn from the headings, labels, and names?
- Which accessibility or WCAG-informed check did you perform?
- What would you change if the content became longer or the image failed to load?

### Questions about CSS and visual design

- Why did you choose Flexbox, Grid, or another layout approach?
- What happens at a viewport width you did not design for directly?
- How did you check contrast, text size, spacing, or reflow?
- Which style is shared, and which style belongs only to this component?
- What would you change if the user zoomed in or increased the text size?

### Questions about JavaScript and modules

- What starts the code running, and what happens next?
- Which function is responsible for each part of the process?
- What data does this function receive and return?
- What happens if the user provides unexpected input?
- How could you reuse this code in another page or feature?

### Questions about APIs and asynchronous code

- What happens while the request is in progress?
- How do you know whether the response succeeded?
- What does the code do when the response is empty or malformed?
- What evidence did you inspect in the Network panel?
- How would the interface behave if the API were temporarily unavailable?

### Questions about state, URLs, and interactions

- Where is the current state stored, and what changes it?
- What happens after a refresh or direct visit to this URL?
- What happens when the identifier or saved data is missing or invalid?
- How do the visual state and accessibility state stay synchronized?
- How would this work if the page had two copies of the interaction?

### Questions about debugging and AI use

- What was the original symptom, and what did you expect instead?
- What evidence led you to your root-cause hypothesis?
- Which possible causes did you rule out?
- How did you verify that the fix worked and did not create a regression?
- If you used AI, what did it suggest, what did you reject, and how did you verify the final result?

## Meet with the instructor or TA

Request the formal pass-off only after you have reviewed every slice with a peer and completed the peer review record. Be ready to complete a short demonstration of the competency as a whole. The instructor or TA may ask you to:

- connect two or more slices in the same workflow,
- explain the relevant code or design,
- demonstrate representative behavior,
- show tests or verification results from across the competency,
- describe a challenge and its solution,
- make a small change,
- predict what will happen in an edge case, or
- diagnose a deliberately introduced small problem.

The purpose is not to surprise you with unrelated material. The questions should stay connected to the competency and the slices you reviewed.

## What counts as passing?

A competency is ready for formal pass-off when you have reviewed every slice with a peer and can:

- demonstrate representative behavior from the competency,
- explain the important concepts and decisions from all of its slices in your own words,
- provide evidence that you tested or verified the relevant results,
- describe limitations, challenges, or tradeoffs,
- connect the slices into a coherent workflow, and
- respond appropriately to a small variation or follow-up question.

You may be asked to revise and try again. “Not yet” is useful feedback, not a failure of the entire course. Record which slice or whole-competency connection is missing, choose the smallest next step, and schedule another review when ready.

## Guidance for a successful conversation

### For the student explaining

- Start with the goal before opening the code.
- Use your own words and explain the reason behind important choices.
- Show a real example instead of making broad claims.
- Say what you do not know; uncertainty gives you and your peer something useful to investigate.
- Keep the scope focused. A clear explanation of one slice is stronger than a rushed tour of an entire project.

### For the peer listening

- Ask questions to understand, not to prove that you know more.
- Give the student time to think before offering an answer.
- Ask “why?” and “what would happen if...?” when the explanation is only a description of steps.
- Point to specific evidence when giving feedback.
- End with one strength and one suggested next step.

## Pass-off record

After the meeting, complete a short record:

```md
Competency: [Name]
Slices reviewed with peer: [List every slice]
Date: [Date]
Peer conversation partner: [Name]
Instructor or TA: [Name]

What I demonstrated across the competency:

[Brief description]

What my peer helped me notice:

[Brief description]

Evidence I showed for the slices:

- [Artifact]
- [Test or verification]
- [Explanation, challenge, or decision]

Peer review status: [Every slice reviewed / More peer review needed]

Competency pass-off result: [Passed / Not yet / Pass with next step]

My next step:

[Specific next action]
```

The record is part of your learning history. It helps you choose the next contract based on evidence rather than guesswork.
